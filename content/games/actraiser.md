## The Game

ActRaiser arrived in Japan in December 1990, barely a month behind the Super
Famicom itself, and it does not behave like a launch-window game. Launch games
are usually a safe idea executed loudly. This one is two entirely separate
games sharing a cartridge, and it never really explains itself.

Half of it is a side-scrolling action game. You inhabit a stone statue, swing a
sword with a satisfying amount of weight behind it, and work through themed
stages that end in a boss. It is deliberate rather than fast — closer to
*Castlevania*'s measured, committed swings than to anything twitchy — and the
hit detection assumes you are thinking a step ahead.

The other half is a god game. Between action stages you hover above a region as
a disembodied presence, guide a small population toward building roads and
houses, clear obstacles with earthquakes and lightning, and watch a settlement
slowly fill in the map. It is not deep. What it is, is *connected*: the
population you grow feeds directly back into your health and abilities in the
action half, so the two modes keep handing each other momentum.

The framing that ties it together is the reason it works. You are a god
recovering territory from six regional demons, and the two modes are simply the
two things a god does — smiting in person, and quietly tending to the people in
between. Very few games since have committed to a premise this cleanly.

## The People

ActRaiser was made by **Quintet**, a studio founded by ex-Nihon Falcom staff,
and published by **Enix** — the *Dragon Quest* company, several years before the
merger with Square. Quintet went on to make *Soul Blazer*, *Illusion of Gaia*
and *Terranigma*, and the through-line from ActRaiser to all three is obvious in
hindsight: creation, restoration and repopulation as mechanics rather than as
backdrop. This is the game where that idea starts.

Direction came from **Masaya Hashimoto**, with **Tomoyoshi Miyazaki** on
scenario — the pairing that anchored most of Quintet's output.

The soundtrack is by **Yuzo Koshiro**, and it is the part of ActRaiser that
travelled furthest. Koshiro was already known for the *Ys* games, and what he
wrote here — orchestral, layered, unashamedly grand — became one of the standard
demonstrations of what the SNES sound hardware could actually do. Plenty of
people who have never played ActRaiser know "Fillmore".

## The Press

The reviews at the time were strong, and the interesting thing is *where* they
were strong. The action stages got respectful notices. The music got raves.
The sim half is where opinion splits — some reviewers treated it as the whole
point, others as the bit to get through before the next sword level.
